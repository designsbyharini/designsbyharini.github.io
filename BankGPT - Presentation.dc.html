The problem

Banking apps are built around menus. People think in questions, and money makes them anxious.

What I found

People trust an assistant more when it gets things done quickly and clearly. Speed was one of the strongest trust signals in the research.

What I decided

The AI talks, but every answer arrives as a widget: the numbers you need, one clear action, and a way back.

Where it went

One widget language covered nine money moments and 20+ open questions. The concept was shown at the Connected Banking Summit, Ethiopia 2026.

01Where it started

Millions of people already talk to their bank. Most assistants still reply like a help centre.

Large banks already have chat assistants. The best known is Erica, Bank of America’s in-app virtual assistant, launched in 2018. You can ask Erica things like “what did I spend on groceries?” and it answers inside the app. Wells Fargo has a similar one called Fargo. Their numbers show people are happy to bank by asking:

What I took from Erica

Before designing anything, I studied what Erica actually does well. Six of its features shaped BankGPT directly.

01Proactive insights

Erica warns people when their spending pattern may take their balance below zero. Today, about half of all Erica conversations start with a suggestion from Erica, not a question from the customer.

In BankGPT

Today’s reminders on the home screen, and the “You may run low before month end” forecast widget.

02A weekly spending snapshot

Erica’s Spend Path gives a weekly view of where money went.

In BankGPT

“How am I doing this month?” and the statement analysis widget.

03Watching recurring charges

Erica flags when a subscription price goes up, spots duplicate charges and confirms refunds.

In BankGPT

The AI Insight line on transaction widgets, and the opportunity nudge, kept for moments that matter.

04Tap, voice or text

People can use Erica by tapping, speaking or typing.

In BankGPT

The same widgets come back whether you type or speak.

05Lock and unlock a lost card

Erica can switch a lost card off, and back on if it turns up.

In BankGPT

The “Block my card” flow: temporary or permanent, with “You can unblock this anytime.”

06Handing over to a person

Bank of America connects people from Erica to a human specialist who picks up where the chat left off.

In BankGPT

The “never lose progress” principle. A human handoff isn’t designed yet; it’s on my next list.

Sources: Bank of America Erica product page and newsroom; Banking Dive; CX Dive; The Motley Fool.
What I started with

A broad brief: make everyday banking easier with AI. There was also an early build of the assistant we could test.

What I expected

That we needed a smarter chatbot. Better understanding, more tasks supported.

What changed my mind

The research kept pointing at feelings, not features: worry, reassurance, control. A smarter chatbot doesn’t fix any of those.

The question I kept asking

How can a conversational AI become a trusted, easy way to do everyday banking?

03Research

The reading that shaped every screen.

There was no budget for interviews, so I went deep on published research instead. I gathered it into one long research log, then traced each finding back to the original study before using it. Every claim below has a source, and every source changed something in the design.

University of Birmingham
Keeping it Human: Public Attitudes Towards AI in Banking
What it found

People deal with banking apps emotionally. Money creates anxiety and fear of mistakes. People check their balance repeatedly for reassurance. Trust comes from predictability, and people want control even when AI helps.

What it meant for BankGPT

Reassure after every action. Behave the same way every time. Never move money without a confirmation.

Shows up in: balance widget, confirm and success widgets
ScienceDirect
The Adoption of Conversational Assistants in Banking Services
What it found

People don’t type structured commands. They write fragments like “send money to John” and expect the assistant to understand, ask follow-up questions and offer quick options.

What it meant for BankGPT

Show suggested replies after every answer. Ask when unsure instead of guessing. Never leave an empty chat screen.

Shows up in: chips, the “Which Mike?” question
Nature
Role of AI-Activated Positive Emotions in Digital Financial Services
What it found

In a study of 400 banking users, positive emotions increased continued use of AI financial tools. When people felt guided and understood, they trusted the tool and came back.

What it meant for BankGPT

End every task on a positive note (“Done! You’re all set.”). Offer a small insight, not just an answer.

Shows up in: AI Insight line, success messages
Speed builds trust
The quicker it works, the more people trust it.

In the Nature study, people who found the assistant quick and efficient trusted it noticeably more. It was one of the strongest links in the whole study.

More than half
56%

Efficiency explained more than half (55.7%) of why people stayed satisfied and kept using the assistant. So I focused on making answers quick and clear before making them clever.

What surprised me

I went in thinking trust would come from branding and security badges. The research kept saying it comes from something simpler: how quickly people get a clear answer.

04Landscape

Banking assistants are moving through four stages. We aimed for the third.

Stage 1
FAQ chatbot

Answers scripted questions.

Stage 2
Assistant

Understands requests and completes tasks.

Stage 3 · BankGPT
Proactive companion

Guides you and keeps an eye on things before you ask.

Stage 4
Financial agent

Acts on your behalf.

Each step up needs more trust and more personal data. Jumping straight to an agent that moves money by itself would skip trust people haven’t given yet.

Revolut AIR

AI as a main way into a banking app, not a side feature.

So the assistant can be the front door.

Google Maps

Directions don’t reset when you switch from typing to voice.

So switching input should never lose progress.

Uber and Amazon

Chat and screens share the same task.

In banking, assistants usually hand you back to menus to finish.

06Why widgets

So I decided BankGPT would answer with widgets, not paragraphs.

A widget is a small interactive card inside the chat. It shows the key numbers, one main action and a way to change your mind. Each part traces back to something the research or our testing told us.

People check their balance again and again for reassuranceBirmingham study
→The key number is the biggest thing on the card, readable in one glance.
The quicker and smoother it works, the more people trust itNature study
→One glance, one action. No paragraph to read before you can act.
People don’t type structured commandsScienceDirect study
→Chips under every widget offer the next words, so no one faces a blank box.
Money creates fear of mistakesBirmingham study
→Amount, payee and account sit together, visible before you confirm.
People want control, even when AI helpsBirmingham study
→Edit, cancel and undo live inside the widget, not in a menu somewhere else.
“All responses are given as Texts and not as widgets”Our testing of the early build, shown next
→Every answer type got a widget, from balance to bill payment.
Option A
Chat that replies in text

The familiar chatbot. Flexible and quick to build.

Why not: numbers get buried in sentences. Slow to scan and hard to trust, exactly what the research warned about.

Option B
Classic app with an AI tab

Keep the menus and add a chatbot on the side.

Why not: two separate worlds. Tasks restart when you switch, which breaks the “never lose progress” principle.

Option C · chosen
Conversation with widgets

The AI talks, and the answer is an interactive card inside the thread.

The cost: more design work per scenario, and every widget needs states. We accepted that.

The answer, part by part
01
A short reply that sets context“Sure. I can help you pay your electricity bill.” It shows the AI understood before anything else happens.
02
Chips to correct it earlyPick the provider before anything is filled in. If the AI guessed wrong, one tap fixes it.
03

Chips for what comes nextDownload the receipt, save the biller, or undo. There is always a way forward or back.
05
A closing line“Done! You’re all set.” It’s a small line, but it tells people they can stop worrying.

17Where my work goes against the research

Not everything lines up. Here’s where my own screens go against the research.

I’d rather point these out than have someone find them. Each one is a real tension in the current design.

The research said

Keep nudges for important moments only.

What my screens do

The final home shows a bill, a KYC deadline and a savings goal at once, plus three chips.

Why it happened

I wanted the home to feel proactive, and each reminder felt important on its own.

What I’d do next

Show one top priority and fold the rest under “See all”, then test whether people notice less but act more.

The research said

Never move money without a confirmation.

What my screens do

“If I get paid, move TZS 150,000” moves money later, without asking each time.

Why it happened

Automation is where the real value is, and stage 3 is about acting ahead of time.

What I’d do next

Keep the one-time review and “edit or stop anytime”, and add a heads-up before each run.

The research said

Switching from typing to voice should keep the task.

What my screens do

The wireframes cover voice, but the final screens don’t show a voice handoff mid-task.

Why it happened

We prioritised text flows for the summit demo.

What I’d do next

Design the voice state for each widget and test a handoff in the middle of a payment.

The research said

Trust comes from being open and predictable.

What my screens do

Version 3 said “AI may make mistakes. Verify details.” The final home replaced it with “End-to-end encrypted”.

Why it happened

The disclaimer felt alarming next to money.

What I’d do next

Test both. People may trust an honest note more than a reassuring one.

18Trade-offs

What I chose, and what I gave up for it.

Safety over speed

A confirmation step adds a tap to every payment. With money, I chose fewer mistakes over fewer taps.

Advice over automation

The AI suggests but rarely acts alone. Stage 4 has to be earned.

Consistency over novelty

Every answer uses the same five parts, even where a custom layout might look nicer.

Fewer nudges, read more often

Proactive messages are kept for important moments, so the ones that appear get read.

19Takeaways

What I learned, and how far we’ve come.

1
Iterations matter more than I imagined.

Each iteration and each new understanding of the requirements is what brought us here. Six rounds of wireframes and five home screens later, the final design looks nothing like where we started.

2
Research and behavioural understanding shape the experience.

Knowing that people check their balance for reassurance changed what the balance widget shows. That came from reading, not from guessing.

3
Every app serves different kinds of users.

The repeater, the explorer and the planner all use the same assistant in different ways. All of them need to be considered.

4
The journey is where I found the friction.

Walking through Ashley’s Tuesday night showed problems that never appeared when I looked at single screens.

5
The simplest language matters most in banking.

“Safe to spend” works better than “available discretionary balance”. When money is involved, people shouldn’t have to decode anything.

6
Testing what exists is the fastest way to convince a team.

Putting research findings next to real screens from the early build changed minds faster than any presentation.

7
The tricky cases take as long as the happy path.

Two Mikes, an unknown biller, a landlord who isn’t saved yet. Designing these took as much time as the main flows, and they’re where trust is won or lost.

8
Being honest about gaps makes the next step clear.

Listing where my screens still go against the research gave us a ready-made list of what to test next.
