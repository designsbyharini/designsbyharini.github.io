01 · Context

Offline, nobody asks you to pick a package.

Urban Company gave me a brief: lots of people open home cleaning every week, start going through the choices, and drop off somewhere in the middle.

The decision

It’s a big one

It costs a fair amount, takes a few hours, and means letting someone into your home.

Offline

You describe your house

Someone understands it, and you get one price that feels right. The same people book this way without trouble.

In the app

You configure it yourself

A list of packages, then a screen of options. This gap became the thread I followed.

02 · Constraints

I could change the words and the order. Nothing else.

I couldn't change

Prices
What can be customised
What each package includes and excludes
Discounts, operations, anything after booking

I could change

The communication
The order things are shown in
Anything of that nature

Urban Company’s thinking

“If we simply recommended the best option by pre-selecting it, the whole thing would feel much easier.”

I didn’t take it as it was. Pre-selecting a package without knowing the home is still a guess. So I set out to have the app understand the home first, and recommend after that.

03 · Understanding

So I booked a cleaning myself, screen by screen.

The whole journey was too big to fix at once. I zoomed in on the part where people leave.

User story

As a user, I want to book a home cleaning service with a suitable package without abandoning the booking process.

The early steps felt easy. Then came the customise step.

Picking a category and a service was clear. Choosing a home type made me pause. Customising the package is where the choices piled up: home size, appliances, add-ons and a long “What is included” list, all on one screen.

The problem

People know what they want cleaned. They aren’t sure which package means that. “My kitchen is greasy” isn’t “Furnished apartment – Home deep cleaning”.

05 · Goals

I set three goals before I designed anything.

Business goal

Increase booking completion by helping people choose with confidence.

Experience goal

Help users find the right existing package before uncertainty turns into drop-off.

User goal

“I want to know which package fits my home without getting confused by the listings.”

What I’d measure
Booking completion rateDrop-off at the package stepAverage order value
Users feel less confident about what they’re booking.→When they clearly understand what’s included, they book with more confidence.
Users compare prices instead of outcomes.→When they see the value delivered, not only the price, they’re more willing to finish.
Users don’t know what they miss in the selected package.→When they know what’s excluded, there’s less uncertainty.

Version 1

My first idea: tell people exactly what they’re getting.

I added a “Before you book” block to the cart that lists what you get and what isn’t included.

Why I dropped it

The cart already lists what’s included. This repeated information people could already find, and it came too late to help them pick.

Version 2

My second idea: let them put two packages side by side.

“Add to compare” on the listing, and a table for price, duration, who it’s for and which areas are covered.

Why I dropped it

If people don’t know which package fits, they won’t know which two to compare either.

Version 3 · the one I kept

Third time round, I stopped adding information and started listening.

I went back to the offline clue in the brief. What if the app let people describe their home, the way they would on a phone call?

07 · Feedback

I shared the draft on LinkedIn. My peers asked the right questions.

Every question below changed something in the final design, or confirmed a call I had already made.

Read the LinkedIn post →
01

What if the user leaves out crucial information?

Added · playback summary

I turned the description into “Here’s what we understood”, so a missing detail is easy to spot and fix before any recommendation.

02

What if they only type “clean my house pls”?

Added · quick questions

When the description is thin, the app asks four tap-to-answer questions, with a line on why it’s asking. The recommendation waits until it has enough.

03

Two input fields on the home screen?

Moved · input location

I moved the input into the cleaning category screen. By then the user has chosen cleaning, and it comes before the package list where the confusion starts.

04

How would users edit their requirements after the conversation?

Added · edit everywhere

Edit sits on the summary and on the recommendations, and “Go back” on the confirmation screen. The app structures the need, the user can always correct it.

05

Doesn’t the cart already list what’s included?

Confirmed · dropping V1

It does. This matched why I had already dropped Version 1. The note on showing which home size a price is for is one I’ve kept for the next iteration.

08 · Solution

It starts with “How can we help you today?”

People type or speak their need. The app plays back what it understood. Once they confirm, they see one best-value package with the reason it fits, then the other options.

09 · Reflection

The journey map did more work than any screen I designed.

01

The journey is where I understood the problem

I could have gone straight to screens. Instead I booked a cleaning myself and wrote down every decision I made. The early steps were fine. The customise step was where the choices piled up. Without the map, I would have redesigned the wrong screen.

02

Iterating beat my first idea

Versions 1 and 2 weren’t wasted. Each one answered a question: people could already see what’s included in the cart, and comparing only works once you have a shortlist. Dropping them is what pointed me to letting people describe their home.

03

Feedback found what I couldn’t see

I was too close to my own idea to spot its gaps. My peers asked what happens with vague input, missing details, and edits after the fact. Each of those questions became a part of the final flow.

10 · Learnings

What I’m taking into my next project.

01

Scope before solving

Cutting the full journey down to the part where people leave made the problem small enough to design for.

02

Question the suggested solution

Pre-selecting sounded easy, but without knowing the home it’s still a guess. Understanding first made the recommendation earn its place.

03

Constraints can help

With prices and packages fixed, I could only change the words and the order. That kept me focused on communication, not new features.

04

Design the unhappy path early

The vague-input case ended up as half of the solution, not an edge case.

05

Share while it’s rough

Posting a draft got me better questions than polishing it alone would have.

06

Test with real users next

Peer feedback shaped this version. The next step is watching real users try both paths before trusting the idea.
